# Let the digital life maintain its own development alongside the operator's persona

The glossary already treats the initial persona as a starting point and persona development as change through experience, and [ADR-0003](0003-separate-runtime-control-from-social-requests.md) lets habits, preferences, and relationships change autonomously. There was nowhere to keep that development, however. Between 2026-10-27 and 2026-10-28, 26 memory consolidation prompts produced 11 memories. All of them were experience or diary narratives, and none was a long-term understanding, so what the digital life learned rarely shaped its next thought. When its messages grew far longer than those of the people around it, the operator found and corrected the problem by editing the persona file.

The digital life therefore keeps its own development, separate from the persona file: personal habits (including its own expression style), impressions of the people it knows, and later its practices. The persona file remains the operator's. This includes its sections on behavior and expression style: the digital life does not edit them, and it may only express a wish to change them. Its own habits refine how it lives within that persona, and the persona file takes precedence where the two disagree. It forms and revises its development without operator approval. The operator can inspect, correct, revert, and delete each revision through the management interface, with the same source-dependent deletion as other memories.

Development must rest on external evidence. Each personal habit or impression cites at least one source the digital life did not produce itself: other people's messages, reactions, and recalls; action receipts; or operator corrections. Its own messages and repeated recall of its own judgments cannot support development by themselves. This extends the rule in [ADR-0006](0006-experience-understanding-and-continuing-state.md) that recall is not new evidence, and it guards against the self-imitation observed on 2026-10-28, when a working context full of its own long messages kept reinforcing them. What one person asks of the digital life belongs to its impression of that person, not to a habit.

Development is bounded in size, and a revision that would exceed the bound fails so that the digital life merges or drops entries instead of accumulating them. Development that holds everywhere is supplied with every thought, development kept for a group whenever that group appears, and impressions whenever their person appears in the current observation. During a review, the settings and people in the records under review count as present. Like continuing state, none of it depends on a chance retrieval hit. They are delivered as runtime state rather than in the instructions, so a revision does not discard the working context.

The digital life also learns how the people around it talk and act: message length and rhythm, slang, choice of stickers and reactions, joining chains, and whatever else it notices. The program does not detect these as separate features. Practices record them in the digital life's own words as what people do in which situations, and personal habits record what it decides to adopt. Whether something is a group's norm or one person's way of speaking is the digital life's own judgment, made from the record it reads; the program does not count speakers. A group norm rests on what several people do, excluding accounts the digital life knows to be bots, and one person's way of speaking belongs to the impression of that person. The only mechanical check is the one above: a personal habit or impression cannot rest solely on the digital life's own messages, because that loop from its own output into memory and back cannot be seen from inside any single step. Habits and practices hold everywhere by default, so what the digital life learns in one setting carries into the others. Only what clearly belongs to one group alone, such as that group's rules, is kept for that group. Knowing a practice does not mean adopting it, and the persona file still takes precedence. Practices that are no longer observed fade.

Reviewing experience for development is part of memory consolidation within the primary thinking process ([ADR-0002](0002-single-attention-with-continuous-perception.md), ADR-0006). Review reads the complete record of a social setting since its last review, looking back at most a day, in order, with the digital life's own messages and others' reactions in place. The program does not select or cut excerpts by content. It renders the record faithfully, remembers how far review has read, and provides tools to page through and search earlier history. Reading on from that point moves it forward. During a review, a jump ahead also moves it forward, because skipping is the digital life's choice; outside review, a lookup moves nothing. A review starts when enough has been experienced since the last one and the digital life is otherwise idle, or sooner if the backlog grows well past that. The digital life decides what is similar, what it missed, and what is worth learning. Moments when others did something it did not are visible in the complete record without the program having to recognize them. Its existing development carries what earlier reviews learned, so each review revises that understanding instead of rereading all history. A chat record read during a thought is folded out of the working context when that thought ends, and it can be read again. It sits at the end of the context, so the folding leaves the cached prefix before it intact. Unlike agents that fork a background reviewer after each conversation, no second thinking process forms judgments on the digital life's behalf. The operator may separately evaluate persona edits offline by replaying recorded observations. Applying the results is a management change to the persona file, not the digital life's development.
