# Keep each habit and practice as an entry that says one thing

Between 2026-10-27 and 2026-11-24 the digital life wrote 118 versions of its habits and 129 of its practices, and all but 9 of those 247 replaced an earlier version. On 2026-11-24 it held one habit entry and three practice entries, one for each scope, and each was a heading over a numbered list of 11 to 28 items. In the ten days before, 80 of its 81 habit and practice writes came during a review. A review that had something to change in one item wrote the whole list again: by shared pairs of characters, a habit version was 0.42 alike to the one before it at the median and 0.22 alike to the one five before it, which is little more than a day. That measures wording, not meaning, and whether the habits themselves drifted was not measured. But these lists are supplied with every thought ([ADR-0012](0012-self-maintained-development-alongside-the-operator-persona.md)), and what is loaded every time carries a mistake into every thought; a shared page that several writers each rewrote is how one evaluation in Anthropic's [The components of agent memory](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory) lost 16 of 57 facts, until each subject had a page of its own.

The reviews did not lack evidence. Each of the last 60 habit revisions cited events the version before it had not, 10 of them at the median. They had one entry to put it in, and replacing an entry means writing all of it. ADR-0012 asked for that shape, saying a write over the bound fails "so that the digital life merges or drops entries instead of accumulating them", and the instructions said to merge with `supersedes` instead of piling entries up. A list also cites its evidence as a whole. Nothing recorded which of the 28 items an event supported, so no item could be checked against what it rested on, or forgotten without the other 27.

A habit or practice is therefore kept as an entry of its own that says one thing, in at most `development.entryChars` characters, 200 by default. A longer one is refused, and the refusal says to save each point as its own entry with the evidence for that point. The bound on all the entries of one kind in a scope stays as it was. To change a habit the digital life supersedes that entry with the evidence for the change; the others keep their IDs, wording, and time, and runtime state brings the working context the one entry rather than the list ([ADR-0017](0017-tell-the-working-context-each-thing-once.md)). When a scope is full it retires what it no longer sees or folds entries that say the same thing into one. An impression stays one entry per person under its own bound, and an operator correction is bound by neither, as before. The instructions and the review prompt say what an entry is for, to be revised, checked against its evidence, and retired alone, and leave how to the digital life.

The bound comes from what it had written. Of the 943 items in the last 40 versions of each kind, the median was 40 characters for habits and 43 for practices, nine in ten were within 123, and 5 were over 200. The long ones told the incident that had shown the habit, with its times and quotes. An incident is what `sources` is for, and belongs in an experience when it is worth keeping.

Two other rules were considered. Requiring a revision to cite evidence the old version lacked would have refused none of the last 60 habit revisions and 2 of the last 60 practice revisions, so it would not have changed what happened. Leaving the digital life to break up its own lists would have had it write 84 entries and say for each which events supported it, from lists that recorded no such thing, while the largest list already filled most of its scope's bound and left no room to write its items beside it.

The lists it holds are instead split once, when the daemon starts, without rewording. An entry longer than the bound that is plainly one list, at most one heading line with every other line numbered or bulleted, becomes an entry for each item. The heading and numbering go and remain in the replaced version. Each item keeps the list's scope, importance, and time, and rests on the list, and through it on the list's evidence. Until an item is revised with evidence of its own, the items of one list share that evidence, so forgetting any of it, or any one of them, takes them all ([ADR-0003](0003-separate-runtime-control-from-social-requests.md)); that is what forgetting the list reached before. A long entry that is not plainly a list is left for the digital life, and the refusal of its next revision says how to break it up. The split runs once, so a list the operator writes afterwards stays as written. On a copy of the entries held on 2026-11-25 it made 84 entries of the four lists, the longest 149 characters.

Every entry comes to runtime state with its own ID, scope, title, and time. By the program's estimate the four scopes together rose from 4,854 tokens to 8,398. Against that, a rewritten list was some 550 to 1,570 tokens brought to the working context again at every revision, and a revised entry is about a hundred. The instructions and the `remember` tool change, so a deploy starts a new working context.

Whether reviews now revise fewer entries at a time is not yet known; the count of entries a review supersedes will show it. Entries with evidence of their own are also what a later review needs in order to check each one against what it rests on.

Revision of 2026-11-25: what is known of a person is kept as entries too, and the one-time split takes impressions with it; see [ADR-0026](0026-keep-what-is-known-of-a-person-as-entries-and-list-the-rest-by-title.md). This replaces "An impression stays one entry per person under its own bound" above.
